# Representative strumming patterns and stroke direction from audio (state of practice, 2026)

Scope: how software derives one strumming pattern for a section from many bars, how it decides the pattern is reliable, and how (or whether) down/up stroke direction is measured from audio. Written for a reviewer comparing against a pipeline that (a) quantises onsets to 8th/16th slots, (b) votes a pattern by slot-wise majority unless a Jaccard medoid bar wins by a margin, (c) allows two-bar medoids, (d) marks uncertainty by a within-bar shuffle test, and (e) assigns direction by slot parity (on-beat = down, off-beat = up).

Research method note: most primary sources were PDFs that had to be text-extracted locally (ISMIR 2004, ISMIR 2014, ISMIR 2015, Sioros & Guedes 2014); the Chordify support article is behind a Cloudflare challenge and was read through a headless browser. Quotations below are short and taken from those extracted texts.

## Key question 1: How do Chordify, Moises, Yousician, Songsterr, Ultimate Guitar, Soundslice, Klangio and research prototypes derive a strumming pattern for a section?

### Takeaway
No consumer product claims to derive a section strumming pattern from audio for arbitrary songs: Chordify says outright it cannot and will not; Ultimate Guitar's and Soundslice's patterns are human-authored; Songsterr's AI transcribes notes but "can't see how to strum"; Moises has chords with bars and beats but no strumming feature. The only published audio-to-pattern system for commercial use is Yousician's WASPAA 2025 paper (Lukoianov & Klapuri), which decodes a whole-song pattern sequence from a 924-pattern expert vocabulary by Viterbi search, with per-song transition costs that make the pattern stick, rather than voting per section; it does not predict direction.

### Cited Findings
- Chordify support (article dated 2021-04-19, still live): "Unfortunately, we're not able to accurately detect strumming patterns on Chordify - this is near-impossible to detect accurately with algorithms, so it is unlikely this would be added in the future." It recommends the Loops feature instead — [Chordify Support](https://support.chordify.net/hc/en-us/articles/360019489618-Does-Chordify-show-the-strumming-patterns-)
- Chordify Song Lessons (a newer feature) split practice into chords, chord transitions and a strumming pattern; the lessons are curated content rather than audio analysis — [Chordify Support: What are Song Lessons?](https://support.chordify.net/hc/en-us/articles/33951891429917-What-are-Song-Lessons)
- Moises' chord detection shows chords synced with bars and beats, at three difficulty levels (Easy/Medium/Advanced); no strumming-pattern feature is described in its feature pages — [Moises blog: advanced chord detection](https://moises.ai/blog/latest/advanced-chord-detection/); [Moises features](https://moises.ai/blog/moises-news/moises-features/)
- Ultimate Guitar: "All Official Tabs are created by the Ultimate Guitar team"; community tabs are user-submitted and moderated — [UG Help: Tabs](https://help.ultimate-guitar.com/en/articles/6741306-tabs)
- UG strumming notation: arrows for direction, small X above an arrow = muted strum, big X in place of an arrow = palm mute, ">" = accent, empty space = rest; "Strumming patterns are not present in all tabs but we are gradually adding them"; the page does not say whether the pattern is per section or per song — [UG Help: Strumming patterns](https://help.ultimate-guitar.com/en/articles/6744662-strumming-patterns-how-to-read-play)
- Soundslice treats strum direction as a per-note notation: select a note, open "Performance", click "Toggle strum upward/downward"; the arrows sit between tab and standard notation. It is a manual editor feature (announced June 2018), not analysis — [Soundslice help: strum directions](https://www.soundslice.com/help/en/creating/notations/337/strum-directions/)
- Songsterr's AI tab generation (heise, 17 Apr 2025): detects notes, string/fret position, vibrato, harmonics; "Palm muting is often ignored"; "the AI can't see – yet? — exactly how to finger or strum strings"; works only on YouTube uploads, 20 s clips without premium — [heise](https://heise.de/-10356152)
- Songsterr also hosts a human-tabbed "Strumming Patterns Tabs" collection — [Songsterr](https://www.songsterr.com/a/wsa/strumming-patterns-tabs-a25846)
- Yousician (Lukoianov & Klapuri, "Transcribing Rhythmic Patterns of the Guitar Track in Polyphonic Music", accepted WASPAA 2025, arXiv 7 Oct 2025): expert musicians transcribed rhythmic patterns for 410 popular songs (931 recordings at up to four difficulty levels); the three steps are HTDemucs separation (the "other" stem worked best), a fine-tuned MERT-v1-95M + MLP per-frame strum detector (peaks > 0.5 within ±40 ms), and Viterbi decoding of the strum sequence into patterns from an expert-curated vocabulary of 924 distinct patterns, most spanning one or two bars, finest subdivision a 16th — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05756v1); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Yousician decoding details: observation likelihood is a two-way-mismatch variant — each observed strum is matched to its nearest pattern strum and each pattern strum to its nearest observed strum, both squared distances entering a Gaussian term; strum positions are continuous values normalised 0–1 within the measure (no grid snapping); two-measure patterns compare measure m and m+1; empty patterns (one per time signature) cover silent bars; staying on the same pattern costs 0, switching costs c1, switching time signature costs c1+c2 (values not given); a flat prior over patterns (favouring common ones did not help) — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Yousician results: strum-detection F1 96.9 ± 0.2 on the "other" stem vs 83.0 for a librosa peak-picking baseline on the isolated guitar track; reconstructed-strum F1 96.8 with ground-truth bar lines and 94.7 with estimated bar lines (BeatThis + dynamic-programming post-processing); bar-line F1 87.2 at 70 ms tolerance; "pattern discontinuity" (pattern changes / measures) fell from 27.8 % to 15.4 % when transition costs were added, with no accuracy loss; simplified arrangements had the fewest pattern changes (11.9 %) vs 17–19 % for intermediate/advanced/original — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Yousician limits: up/down direction, muted/percussive strokes and syncopation are not addressed; the dataset is proprietary; the companion repo holds only a `downbeat.py` post-processor and eight 10 s excerpts, with no stated licence — [GitHub YousicianGit/rhythmic-pattern-transcription](https://github.com/YousicianGit/rhythmic-pattern-transcription)
- Klangio's Guitar2Tabs offers a user-selected "strumming" mode (vs fingerpicking) and its API lists "chord and strum recognition"; its researcher Sebastian Murgul authored the ISMIR 2025 strumming-direction paper — [MuseHub Guitar2Tabs](https://musehub.com/app/guitar2tabs); [KIT staff page](https://www.iiit.kit.edu/murgul.php)
- Yousician's earlier app behaviour (Engadget review): a scrolling tab plus scoring of whether each chord lands on the beat; transcriptions credited partly to access to master recordings — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/yousician-metallica-guitar-instruction-course-140027954.html)

### Inferences
- The one production-grade audio system (Yousician) avoids both "majority vote" and "pick a bar": it scores every candidate pattern against every bar with a soft, continuous-time mismatch and then lets a hysteresis cost (switch penalty) decide when the pattern really changes. That is a direct alternative to the slot-wise majority / medoid-margin rule: a section's pattern is the one that minimises summed mismatch over its bars plus switch penalties, and a two-bar pattern competes on equal footing because the vocabulary contains both lengths.
- Yousician reports pattern-change rate as a readability metric, which is the closest published analogue to "printed two wrong patterns as certain after fewer bars entered the vote": fewer bars per decision raises discontinuity; their remedy was a transition cost, not a larger margin.
- Every consumer product that shows a direction row gets it from a human.

### Gaps
- No public description of how Yousician's app (as opposed to the paper) chooses the displayed pattern per section, nor Chordify's Song Lessons pipeline.
- No documentation found for Moises or Yousician help centres on strumming; Yousician's support site was not surfaced by search.
- Yousician's transition-cost constants c1, c2 and the Gaussian variance are not published.

## Key question 2: What does the literature say about summarising a set of bar-level onset vectors (clustering, templates, similarity measures, medoid vs centroid, two-bar periodicity)?

### Takeaway
The standard published recipe is cluster-then-prototype, not slot-wise majority: Dixon, Gouyon & Widmer (ISMIR 2004) k-means the per-bar envelopes (k = 4), take the largest cluster and average it, explicitly "to remove outliers"; Yousician matches bars to a fixed vocabulary with a continuous-time two-way mismatch. For binary onset strings, Toussaint's family of measures (Hamming, swap/directed-swap, chronotonic) is the reference set; Hamming/Jaccard penalise a one-slot push as heavily as a distant mismatch, which swap and chronotonic distances do not. Pattern length (1 vs 2 bars) is handled either by putting both lengths in the vocabulary (Yousician) or by reading the first peak of a bar-level self-similarity curve.

### Cited Findings
- Dixon, Gouyon & Widmer 2004: each bar's pattern is the RMS amplitude envelope resampled to b = 72 samples per bar (values 48–144 gave similar results); "In order to remove outliers, k-means clustering (with k = 4) was used to find clusters of similar bars, and the largest cluster was taken as defining the most prominent rhythmic pattern for each piece"; the characteristic pattern is the mean of that cluster; Euclidean distance between patterns — [ISMIR 2004 paper 165](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000165.pdf)
- The same paper flags the distance problem that applies equally to Jaccard on slot vectors: "The Euclidean distance is not necessarily ideal, as it treats all time points independently, so that, for example, peaks which almost match are penalised as heavily as peaks which are far from being aligned", and notes that quantising patterns into musical units would allow matching to "explicit patterns such as those found in instructional books" — [ISMIR 2004 paper 165](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000165.pdf)
- Dixon et al. also observe that extracted patterns show swing (ratio about 2:1 visible in Jive/Quickstep) and syncopation (Rumba accents on the 4th and 6th eighth) directly in the averaged envelope — [ISMIR 2004 paper 165](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000165.pdf)
- Toussaint, "A Comparison of Rhythmic Similarity Measures" (ISMIR 2004): defines Hamming distance (mismatched bits, O(n)), Euclidean inter-onset-interval distance, interval-ratio distance, swap distance ("the minimum number of swaps required to convert one rhythm to another", a swap being an interchange of adjacent 1 and 0; extends to unequal onset counts by requiring every onset of B to receive at least one onset of A), and the chronotonic distance (Gustafson's TEDAS representation — each inter-onset interval drawn as a square — compared with the Kolmogorov variational distance ∫|f1−f2|); "the Hamming, swap, and chronotonic distance measures do not assume this restriction [equal onset count]". In his phylogenetic study the chronotonic distance was "the best measure" — [ISMIR 2004 paper 134](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000134.pdf)
- Listening tests disagree on which measure matches perception: one UPF study found the directed-swap distance best matches human similarity judgements regardless of expertise — [MTG](https://mtg.upf.edu/node/1564); a flamenco study found human judgement matches the chronotonic distance better than directed swap — [Canadian Acoustics](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/download/1893/1640/2030)
- Sioros & Guedes propose organising binary patterns in "syncopation trees" rooted at the de-syncopated pattern, which "can be useful in developing models for clustering rhythms together or in defining measures of rhythmic similarity and distance" — [Sioros & Guedes 2014](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/4/23414/files/2022/01/SiorosGuedes2014.pdf)
- Ellis's "eigenrhythms" work treats aligned bar-length drum patterns as a pattern space and compares PCA with NMF ("posirhythms"), stressing that patterns must be tempo- and downbeat-aligned before modelling — [Ellis 2005 slides](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/talks/musicie-2005-10.pdf)
- Pattern-length estimation from self-similarity: one method compares successive candidate patterns of 1–11 beats and takes the length from the first peak of the similarity curve; typical lengths are 3, 4, 5, 6 or 8 beats — [US patent 7842874](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7842874); diagonal-line search on a thresholded beat-synchronous SSM is the usual way to find repeats — [US patent 9547715](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9547715); Nieto & Farbood split prominent diagonals using perceptual rules on pattern length and rests — [MIREX 2013](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/f/11865/files/2020/08/NietoFarbood-PatDiscoMIREX2013.pdf)
- Yousician: vocabulary patterns are "1 or 2 measures long", a two-measure pattern is scored against measures m and m+1, and the decoder must cover the song without gaps — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- General clustering literature: a medoid "is less influenced by outliers or other extreme values than a mean", and k-medoids minimises pairwise dissimilarities rather than squared Euclidean distances, which is why it is preferred in noisy settings — [arXiv 1509.00692](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.00692); [Wikipedia k-medians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-medians_clustering)

### Inferences
- The pipeline's "slot-wise majority" is the binary analogue of Dixon's cluster mean, but computed over all bars rather than the largest cluster; Dixon's step of discarding minority clusters first is what removes fills, pick-up bars and noisy bars from the average. Majority over all bars flattens syncopation precisely when the pushed bars are a large minority (e.g. 40 %) — the majority slot vector then matches no bar actually played.
- The "medoid by mean Jaccard" is k-medoids with k = 1; its known weakness on short binary vectors is that a single flipped slot in several bars can move the medoid to a noisy bar. Dixon's k = 4 clustering followed by choosing the largest cluster is more robust than a global medoid because a noisy bar cannot be the prototype of the largest cluster unless noise dominates.
- Jaccard/Hamming treat a one-slot anticipation (push) as two errors (missing on-beat, extra off-beat); swap or chronotonic distance counts it as one unit, so a swap-based medoid would not be pulled away from the syncopated reading by a few straight bars. Toussaint's measures are O(n) on 8- or 16-slot strings and are trivial to implement.
- Yousician's data (924 patterns for 410 songs, "most" one or two bars) is evidence that a two-bar vocabulary is normal, not an edge case.

### Gaps
- No paper found that compares medoid vs centroid vs majority specifically for bar-level binary onset vectors; the robustness claims are from general clustering literature.
- No published numbers on what fraction of Yousician's 924 patterns are two-bar.

## Key question 3: How do systems detect pushes/anticipations without inflating strike count, and how do they quantise onsets with swing or drift?

### Takeaway
The formal definition (Longuet-Higgins & Lee; Sioros & Guedes) is that a syncopation is an onset on a weak slot followed by silence on the next stronger slot; the de-syncopation transform moves that onset forward onto the strong slot, which preserves strike count by construction. Yousician avoids hard snapping entirely (continuous positions, Gaussian mismatch); when snapping is used, tolerances in the literature are expressed as a fraction of the beat period (Dittmar et al.) or fixed ±50–70 ms (mir_eval, Yousician bar lines), and swing is measured from autocorrelation rather than from snapped onsets because onset errors propagate into swing estimates.

### Cited Findings
- Longuet-Higgins & Lee define syncopation "as a note onset in a position with a low weight that is not followed by an onset in the following pulse with a higher weight"; weights follow the metrical level each position initiates — [Sioros & Guedes 2014](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/4/23414/files/2022/01/SiorosGuedes2014.pdf)
- Sioros & Guedes reformulate it on the silent slot: "syncopation is found in the silent pulses that belong in strong metrical positions ... and that are preceded by onsets in one of the immediately preceding weaker metrical positions"; de-syncopation "is applied to the silent pulse by shifting the preceding onset" forward; syncopation is generated by "anticipating events in weaker metrical positions"; the shift type is the difference of metrical levels (an eighth-push and a sixteenth-push of the same beat are different types); an onset cannot be shifted past another onset — [Sioros & Guedes 2014](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/4/23414/files/2022/01/SiorosGuedes2014.pdf)
- Metrical template bounds: the fastest subdivision modelled must be ≥ ~100 ms (so the 16th level only exists below ~150 BPM); the slowest level kept is the one in the 500 ms–1 s range; at 160 BPM only three levels survive (750, 375, 187.5 ms) — [Sioros & Guedes 2014](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/4/23414/files/2022/01/SiorosGuedes2014.pdf)
- SynPy (Song, Pearce, Harte, SMC 2015) is a Python toolkit implementing seven syncopation models (LHL, Pressing, Toussaint metric complexity, Sioros–Guedes, Keith, Toussaint off-beatness, Gómez WNBD) per bar with a common interface; bars a model cannot score return None — [SoundSoftware syncopation project](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/syncopation-dataset); [publication](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/publications/155)
- Yousician: strum positions are "continuous values within each measure, scaled between 0 and 1"; the paper "does not describe quantizing onsets to a grid"; bar-line post-processing assumes steady tempo, "which the authors note does not hold for some songs" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Dittmar, Pfleiderer & Müller (ISMIR 2015): onset-based swing estimation accepts an onset triple (backbeat, offbeat, downbeat) only if its span is within a tolerance factor α of the expected beat period ((b_{n+2} − b_n) < α·τ), i.e. tolerance relative to the beat; "small errors in the onset detection propagate considerably into the swing ratio estimates", so they prefer a log-lag autocorrelation (LLACF) that "circumvents the error-prone detection" — [ISMIR 2015 paper 143](https://www.ismir2015.uma.es/articles/143_Paper.pdf)
- Marchand & Peeters (DAFx 2015) estimate swing from the autocorrelation of the onset energy function plus rules, 91 % mean recall, and released GTZAN-rhythm annotations with downbeat, beat and eighth-note positions — [DAFx 2015](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/details/dAxTOO3pHjYwTaMRKMs8PQ)
- Swing ratio definition: ratio of successive eighth-note durations, 1:1 straight, 2:1 triplet; real jazz rarely holds a constant ratio — [MPI DS swing](https://www.ds.mpg.de/swing)
- mir_eval onset matching uses a default 0.05 s window — [mir_eval onset docs](https://mir-eval.readthedocs.io/latest/api/onset.html); Yousician evaluates strums at 50 ms and bar lines at 70 ms — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1); Murgul et al. 2025 evaluate at 50 ms via mir_eval — [arXiv 2508.07973](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07973v1)
- Chord recognisers show a systematic lateness at chord changes; a madmom issue attributes it to noise at downbeats and the model only committing once confident, and reports that aligning chord transitions to the next beat time helped slightly — [madmom issue 403](https://git.tdem.in/CPJKU/madmom/issues/403)

### Inferences
- A pattern that contains an onset on slot "and-of-4" and no onset on the next downbeat is, by the LHL/Sioros definition, a syncopation at eighth level; a bar where the detector found both (push plus a weak re-attack on the downbeat) is the common failure. Sioros' de-syncopation rule gives a deterministic, count-preserving canonicalisation: pushed onset + silent strong slot → treat as the same strike; this is how to vote "push vs straight" without inflating strike count: vote on the de-syncopated root pattern first, then vote separately on which strong slots are anticipated.
- The 100 ms floor means a 16th grid at ≥ 150 BPM is below the perceptual subdivision limit; quantising to 16ths at fast tempi invents syncopation from timing jitter ("syncopation printed where there is none").
- Swing: if the grid is straight 8ths and the player swings 2:1, the off-beat 8th sits at 0.67 of the beat and can snap to the 16th slot after it at 16th resolution, producing spurious 16th syncopation; Dittmar/Marchand suggest estimating a global swing ratio from autocorrelation and warping the grid before snapping.

### Gaps
- No published study gives a recommended snapping tolerance for strumming; values above are evaluation tolerances, not quantisation windows.
- Yousician's Gaussian σ for the mismatch term is unpublished.

## Key question 4: What confidence measures or statistical tests do published systems use to call a pattern reliable?

### Takeaway
No published strumming-pattern system reports a per-pattern significance test; the field uses agreement-style proxies: Yousician's pattern-discontinuity and measure-length discontinuity rates, Holzapfel et al.'s mutual agreement between estimators as a confidence signal, and Dixon's cluster size. A within-bar shuffle test as used in the pipeline has no precedent in this literature; general surrogate-data advice is that naive shuffling destroys autocorrelation and can under-estimate the null's tails.

### Cited Findings
- Yousician readability metrics: pattern discontinuity = pattern changes / measures; time-signature discontinuity; measure-length discontinuity = measures whose length differs by > 35 % from the previous one / measures; transition costs cut pattern discontinuity 27.8 % → 15.4 % — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Holzapfel, Davies, Zapata, Oliveira & Gouyon, "Selective Sampling for Beat Tracking Evaluation" (IEEE TASLP 2012): mutual agreement between beat sequences from a committee of trackers identifies difficult excerpts without ground truth, and the method "can improve beat tracking accuracy on large music collections"; the choice of agreement measure is critical — [INESC TEC listing](https://www.inesctec.pt/pt/publicacoes?page=3357); [MIREX 2012 multi-feature beat tracker](https://investigacion.upb.edu.co/en/publications/infograf%C3%ADa-beat-tracking-task-mirex-2012-multi-feature-beat-track/)
- Dixon et al. use the size of the largest k-means cluster as the de facto reliability of the extracted pattern and discard the other clusters as outliers — [ISMIR 2004 paper 165](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000165.pdf)
- Yousician's strum detector only thresholds at 0.5 probability; "No voting is described ... The only threshold is the 0.5 strum peak-picking threshold" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Surrogate-data caution from spike-train analysis: "simple shuffle bootstraps are not necessarily the best methods if they destroy too much of the auto-correlative structure, and they can severely underestimate the distributional tails" — [arXiv 1806.09963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.09963); permutation tests give p-values with few distributional assumptions — [spynal randstats](https://spynal.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modules/randstats.html)

### Inferences
- The within-bar shuffle null keeps each bar's strike count but destroys metrical position; it therefore tests "are onsets position-dependent at all", which almost any strummed section passes. It does not test "is this particular pattern the right one among close alternatives", which is where the 1.6/1.7 errors occurred. A null that preserves more structure (circular shift of each bar by one slot; or bootstrap over bars and ask how often the winning pattern changes) targets the actual failure.
- The published proxies suggest two cheap confidence signals the pipeline lacks: (1) agreement margin between the top two candidate patterns (Yousician's Viterbi score gap; Holzapfel's agreement), and (2) a minimum-support rule, since Yousician found fewer bars per decision raises pattern-change rate.

### Gaps
- No paper found that reports a calibrated confidence (probability that the displayed pattern is correct) for strumming patterns.
- The full text of Holzapfel et al. 2012 was not retrieved; only abstracts.

## Key question 5: Strum direction from audio — features, accuracies, open implementations

### Takeaway
Direction is recoverable from audio only from the first tens of milliseconds of the strum, because the strings are struck in order (down = low to high pitch, up = high to low). Barbancho et al. (ISMIR 2014) classify direction from the spectral-centroid trajectory across the windows where new partials appear (centroid rises for an up-pitch sweep, falls for down), 91–93 % on guitar chords with 11–55 ms inter-string delays; KIT's audio-only CNN/LSTM reached ~70–72 % on real strumming; the ISMIR 2025 CRNN reached F1 87.9/84.9 (down/up) on pickup audio and 85.5/79.0 on phone-microphone audio with synthetic+real training. Down strokes are consistently easier than up strokes. No open-source model is released; the KIT dataset (Apache-2.0, ~5 min) is public.

### Cited Findings
- Barbancho, Tzanetakis, Tardón, Driessen & Barbancho, "Estimation of the Direction of Strokes and Arpeggios" (ISMIR 2014, CC BY 4.0): "all the information about the movement is contained at the very beginning of the chord waveform. After all the strings of the chord have been played, it is no longer possible to know whether the movement was up or down"; "Because the movement can be fast it is not feasible to look for onsets to detect each note individually" — [ISMIR 2014 paper 130](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000130.pdf)
- Their method: log-spectrogram (44.1 kHz, 1024-sample Hamming window, hop 512, 4096-point DFT); for consecutive frames minimise ε[m] = min_α Σ_k |L_m[k] − α L_{m−1}[k]| (the "old+new heuristic" — subtract the decayed previous spectrum so new partials stand out); peaks of f_{α,ε} = (ε′/max ε′)·|α′/max α′| mark new-string arrivals; the stroke speed and note count come from the roughly constant spacing of those peaks; the direction feature is the spectral centroid of each change window (SPC), classified with Fisher LDA or linear SVM; Fig. 4 shows the centroid rising ~200→400 Hz for the low-to-high (up-pitch) F chord and falling ~460→360 Hz for the high-to-low E chord — [ISMIR 2014 paper 130](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000130.pdf)
- Their results (synthetic chords from RWC guitar samples, inter-string delays 11–55 ms, 100 train / 500 test): guitar SVM success rate 93.29 % with known delays, 91.66 % with estimated delays (up 87.77 %, down 95.55 %) vs 78.61 % for a 15-MFCC baseline (up 58.44 %, down 98.78 %); real autoharp chords (330 recorded): 77.52 % SVM; delay estimation error 13–28 %; code/data "available upon request" — [ISMIR 2014 paper 130](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000130.pdf)
- Terminology caution: Barbancho et al. define UP as "from the lowest to the highest pitch note" — which on a guitar is the physical down-stroke. Reviewers must flip labels when comparing with guitar/ukulele convention — [ISMIR 2014 paper 130](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000130.pdf)
- Physical ordering: "On the downstroke of the guitar, the lowest note sounds first and the highest note sounds last. The upstroke runs the other way" — [MuseScore forum](https://musescore.org/en/node/301189); a sensor-guitar patent states strums "sound different when strummed up instead of down because the strings are hit in a different order" — [US patent 8614389](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8614389)
- Murgul & Heizmann, "A Multimodal Approach to Acoustic Guitar Strumming" (ISMIR 2022 LBD): "Due to the very short time difference between the string hits, conventional note-based transcription methods cannot distinguish the direction"; audio is used for event detection and a hand-mounted 6-axis IMU for direction; test F1 85 % up / 92 % down on a 5-minute labelled recording — [KIT 1000183784](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000183784)
- The same KIT work reports audio-only CNN and LSTM direction classifiers at about 72.5 % and 70 % accuracy — [KIT PDF](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000183784/163349643)
- KLANGIO-GST-MM-T dataset (Apache-2.0): 10 examples, ~5 min, chords C/G/F/Am, line audio via Behringer USB cable + phone audio + synchronised GY-521 IMU CSV + `.strums` annotations; baseline `scripts/evaluate.py` gives down P/R/F1 89.88/94.53/91.94 and up 79.14/93.07/84.65 using motion for direction; no audio-only baseline — [GitHub Klangio/KLANGIO-GST-MM-T](https://github.com/Klangio/KLANGIO-GST-MM-T)
- Murgul, Schimper & Heizmann, "Joint Transcription of Acoustic Guitar Strumming Directions and Chords" (ISMIR 2025): CRNN adapted from Kong et al.'s piano-transcription regression model; input log-mel (16 kHz, window 2048, hop 160, 229 bins); action branch outputs two regression targets (up, down) via biGRU; chord branch 24 major/minor classes; trained ~2 h on a V100 — [arXiv 2508.07973](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07973v1)
- Data: ~90 min real recordings from three guitarists playing 28 patterns in 4/4 at 60/80/100 BPM to a metronome, captured as pickup audio + iPhone 15 Pro microphone + ESP32-S3 wrist IMU; labels from the sign of the acceleration derivative at each spectral-flux onset, then human-corrected; plus ~4 h synthetic audio (51 progressions × 36 patterns on a 16th grid, Ample Sound virtual guitars via DAWDreamer, Pedalboard effects, noise) — [arXiv 2508.07973](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07973v1)
- Results (F1 any / down / up): pickup audio CRNN 97.60 / 87.87 / 84.90, with sensor 97.60 / 90.02 / 88.66; baselines spectral flux 79.49 / 85.40 / 68.60, SuperFlux 74.36 / 84.40 / 67.80, CD-ODF 79.32 / 82.20 / 78.40 (baseline direction presumably from alternation); microphone audio: synthetic-only 89.77 / 73.92 / 52.64, phone-only 85.06 / 79.90 / 66.81, all three sources 92.75 / 85.51 / 79.02; ±6 semitone pitch-shift augmentation helped most; "down-strum F1 is higher than up-strum F1 for all methods"; no code or model released; muted strokes are future work ("pose a challenge for motion-based annotation methods") — [arXiv 2508.07973](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07973v1); [KIT record](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000184108)
- Yousician's rhythmic-pattern system does not predict direction at all — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Reboursière et al. (NIME 2012) detect right-hand techniques using a hexaphonic pickup (one signal per string), which makes string order directly observable but is unavailable from a mixed recording — [NIME 2012](https://nime.org/proc/reboursiere2012)
- Anecdotal timbre differences (down "rich and full bodied", up "thinner") are reported by players but depend on pick slant and are not measured — [Troy Grady forum](https://forum.troygrady.com/t/trapped-vs-escaped-pick-stroke-tone/10867)

### Inferences
- On a Demucs guitar stem of a full mix, the 11–55 ms string-arrival window that carries direction is the part most smeared by separation artefacts and masked by drums; the ISMIR 2025 drop from pickup (87.9/84.9) to phone mic (85.5/79.0) on clean solo recordings suggests mixed-stem accuracy would be lower still. Ukulele's four strings (re-entrant tuning on high-G instruments) further weakens the "low-to-high pitch" cue, since the first string struck on a down stroke is not the lowest-pitched.
- The parity rule (on-beat = down, off-beat = up) is the implicit baseline every paper beats; the ISMIR 2025 baselines that detect onsets and presumably assign direction by alternation score ~85 down / ~68 up, which is roughly the error profile of parity. A measured-direction row would need a model like the CRNN plus training data; none is downloadable.
- A cheap audio feature the pipeline could try for a confidence flag rather than a label: sign of the spectral-centroid slope over the first 3–5 frames after each onset (Barbancho's SPC trajectory), accepting that on ukulele the slope's sign may not map cleanly to hand direction.

### Gaps
- No open-source trained model for strum direction exists; the KIT dataset is ~5 min on four chords.
- No paper reports direction accuracy on ukulele or on source-separated stems.
- The ISMIR 2025 paper is internally inconsistent on the IMU sign convention (positive derivative = up in one place, negative gradient = upward in another).

## Key question 6: How are muted/percussive strokes (chucks, palm mutes) handled in patterns?

### Takeaway
Notation systems treat mutes as a third symbol on the same slot (UG: small X = muted strum, big X = palm mute); audio research treats mute/percussive detection as a per-note technique classification with a damped, short-decay attack as the cue, trained on labelled data (IDMT-SMT-Guitar "muted" plucking style; Stefani's acoustic-guitar classes include Palm Mute and four body-percussion classes). Neither strumming-pattern paper (Yousician, KIT) models mutes; KIT lists them as future work.

### Cited Findings
- UG notation: small X above an arrow = muted strum; big X instead of an arrow = palm mute; empty = rest — [UG Help](https://help.ultimate-guitar.com/en/articles/6744662-strumming-patterns-how-to-read-play)
- Murgul et al. 2025: muted strokes are future work and "pose a challenge for motion-based annotation methods" — [arXiv 2508.07973](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07973v1)
- Yousician: muted or percussive strokes "not addressed" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05756v1)
- Songsterr's AI: "Palm muting is often ignored" — [heise](https://heise.de/-10356152)
- IDMT-SMT-Guitar (Zenodo, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, 1.3 GB): playing-techniques subset of ~4700 note events on three guitars with plucking styles "finger-style, muted, picked" and expression styles incl. dead notes; a chord-and-rhythm subset of 64 short pieces at two tempi on three guitars annotated with onsets, chords, rhythmic pattern length and texture — [Zenodo 7544110](https://zenodo.org/records/7544110); [Fraunhofer IDMT](https://www.idmt.fraunhofer.de/en/publications/datasets/guitar.html)
- Stefani's ExpressiveGuitar-TechniqueClassifier (GPL-3.0): eight classes — Kick (palm on lower body), Snare 1, Tom, Snare 2 ("fingers on the muted strings, over the end of the fingerboard"), Natural Harmonics, Palm Mute ("muting partially the strings with the palm of the pick hand"), Pick Near Bridge, Pick Over the Soundhole (neutral); distributed as feature vectors, not audio; classifier and accuracy not stated on the page — [GitHub](https://github.com/domenicostefani/ExpressiveGuitar-TechniqueClassifier)
- Stefani & Turchet, "Real-time playing technique recognition embedded in a smart acoustic guitar" (EURASIP JASMP 2025): onset detector + feature extractors + convolutional classifier on a Raspberry Pi 4 over eight pitched and percussive techniques; accuracy improves when the latency budget rises from 15 to 45 ms; generalisation across players and instruments is difficult and "individual player styles noticeably affect recognition performance" — [EURASIP JASMP](https://asmp-eurasipjournals.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s13636-025-00413-6)
- TART (technique-aware audio-to-tab) classifies each detected note with a 92-dim frame feature (MFCC + log-mel + chroma at 23 ms hop) over classes including palm muting, kick drum, snare drum and picking/no technique; cross-dataset training showed distribution shift and overfitting — [arXiv 2510.02597](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.02597)
- Reboursière et al. detect palm muting with a hexaphonic pickup (one signal per string) — [NIME 2012](https://nime.org/proc/reboursiere2012)
- Chordify's explanation for not attempting patterns at all is that detection is "near-impossible ... with algorithms" — [Chordify Support](https://support.chordify.net/hc/en-us/articles/360019489618-Does-Chordify-show-the-strumming-patterns-)

### Inferences
- The pattern literature's three-way slot alphabet (struck / muted / rest) matches UG's notation, so the pipeline's slot classes are the right vocabulary; the open question is only the classifier, and every published mute detector is supervised on labelled solo-guitar data — none is validated on separated stems.
- Muted strokes should probably vote with struck strokes on rhythm (they occupy a slot) and separately on type, otherwise a mute/strike disagreement across bars splits the vote and lets a minority reading win.

### Gaps
- No accuracy figures for palm-mute detection were retrievable (Stefani & Turchet full text is paywalled behind a Springer login for WebFetch; Reboursière abstract only).
- No dataset labels ukulele chucks.

## Installability and licensing notes for libraries mentioned

### Takeaway
The useful open pieces install with pip on Windows (beat_this, mir_eval, SynPy-style syncopation code); the research models that matter (MERT strum detector, KIT CRNN) are not released.

### Cited Findings
- beat_this (CPJKU): install PyTorch ≥ 2.0 for your platform, then `pip install tqdm einops soxr rotary-embedding-torch` and `pip install beat-this`; ffmpeg or another torchaudio backend for non-wav — [beat_this README mirror](https://gitblind.noratr.app/CPJKU/beat_this)

- KLANGIO-GST-MM-T: Apache-2.0, dataset + evaluate script, requirements.txt present — [GitHub](https://github.com/Klangio/KLANGIO-GST-MM-T)
- Yousician companion repo: uv project (`uv sync`), `downbeat.process_downbeats(np.float64 array)`; no licence stated — [GitHub](https://github.com/YousicianGit/rhythmic-pattern-transcription)
